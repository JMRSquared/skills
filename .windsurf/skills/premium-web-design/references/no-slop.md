# No slop: the copy standard

Read this before you write a single visible string, and again in the copy pass
(SKILL.md, Step 5b). It applies to every word a visitor can see: headings, body,
buttons, labels, captions, alt text, marquees, FAQ answers, the footer, and the
`<title>`.

## Why this file exists

A page built with this skill shipped these headings for an outdoor advertising
company in Kagiso:

> Already booked. · Five formats. · Before you book. · Seen across Gauteng.

and this line under one of them:

> Book one, or run several on one brief.

The owner called them "too ugly and cryptic". He was right. Each heading needs
the section around it to make sense, and none of them tells a stranger what the
company sells. A business owner reading his own site could not say what "Five
formats." meant without scrolling. A buyer at a procurement desk never will.

That copy contains no clichés. "Elevate", "seamless" and "unlock" appear nowhere
in it. The page still reads as machine-made, because a model imitating an ad
agency writes clipped, knowing fragments the same way a model imitating a
brochure writes "elevate your brand". Both come from the same place: words
chosen for how they sound, with nobody checking what a customer would
understand.

This standard catches both.

## What AI slop is

**AI slop is copy that sounds like marketing and says nothing a customer can
act on.** You can spot it two ways. You can swap in any competitor's name and
the copy still works, or you can show it to a stranger and they cannot tell you
what the business does.

It comes in two families.

### Family A: inflated and generic

The brochure voice. Big words standing in for facts the writer did not have.

- **Buzzwords and puffery.** elevate, unlock, seamless, empower, journey,
  landscape, delve, tapestry, testament, cutting-edge, world-class,
  solutions. The full list with replacements is below.
- **Empty superlatives.** "The best service in Gauteng." Best by what measure,
  according to whom?
- **Significance inflation.** "plays a pivotal role", "stands as a testament
  to", "marks a new era", "deeply rooted in". Wikipedia's AI Cleanup editors
  list these as the strongest content-level sign of generated text.
- **Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features" in
  place of "is" and "has". "Our depot serves as a hub" means "our depot is in
  Kagiso".
- **Tricolons.** Three adjectives, three benefits, three fragments, on every
  block. "Fast. Reliable. Affordable."
- **Negative parallelism.** "Not just a billboard company, but a partner."
  "It's not advertising, it's visibility." "No contracts, no hassle, just
  results."
- **Throat-clearing openers.** "Welcome to", "In today's fast-paced world",
  "Whether you're a small business or a national brand", "Look no further".
- **Rhetorical questions as headings.** "Ready to stand out?" "Why choose us?"
- **Em-dash drama.** "Your brand — everywhere." The single most recognised
  punctuation tell.
- **Fake specificity.** Round numbers nobody measured ("10,000+ happy
  clients", "99% satisfaction"), ratings with no source, "trusted by
  thousands", years in business the brief never gave you.
- **Hedging and filler.** "We strive to", "we aim to provide", "helping you
  to", "designed to help", adverbs (truly, really, simply, seamlessly).
- **Vague attribution.** "Industry experts agree", "clients love us".
- **False ranges.** "From small startups to global brands", "from concept to
  completion".

### Family B: cryptic and clipped

The ad-agency voice, imitated. Short, cool, and undecodable. This is the
failure the Kagiso page shipped, and the one older versions of this skill
encouraged by asking for 2–6 word heroes.

- **Fragment headlines that need context.** "Already booked." "Five formats."
  "Before you book." Each one is the end of a sentence whose start lives in
  the writer's head.
- **Verbless taglines with a full stop.** "Seen across Gauteng." "Built to
  last." The full stop asks the reader to feel weight the words do not carry.
- **One-word and two-word headings.** "Reach." "The work." "Proof." "Now
  showing."
- **Stacked fragments.** "Seen. Remembered. Booked." Three words, three full
  stops, no information.
- **Puns and wordplay.** "Sign of the times." "Board meeting." Puns need the
  reader to already know what you sell, and a stranger does not.
- **Insider jargon.** "OOH", "DOOH", "flighting", "one brief", "formats",
  "placements", "inventory". Industry words a buyer at a spaza shop or a
  municipal office does not use.
- **Clever for its own sake.** Metaphors, poetic inversions, abstract nouns
  ("Presence.", "The craft."), and lines that read well aloud and mean
  nothing on the screen.
- **Teaser questions.** "Ready?" "Who's watching?"
- **Bare imperatives.** "Book." "Start." "Talk to us." Book what? Start what?

Nielsen Norman Group's guidance on web headings sums up why Family B fails:
headings have to work out of context, front-load the keywords, and pick
clarity over cute vocabulary. Visitors scanning a page read the first two or
three words of a heading and decide. "Five formats." spends both words on
nothing.

### Formatting tells

These show up in both families and give a page away before anyone reads it:

- Title Case On Every Heading. Use sentence case.
- Emoji as bullets or decoration.
- An eyebrow label above every section ("OUR SERVICES", "SECTION 02").
- **Bolded lead-ins.** on every paragraph or list item.
- Colon headings: "Billboards: Big Impact." "Speed: Delivered."
- Exclamation marks in headings.
- Three-item lists everywhere, and three-card grids of icon + two words + one
  vague line.
- Full stops on noun-phrase headings ("Our clients.").
- Em dashes anywhere in the copy.

## The rules

### 1. Every heading is a plain statement a stranger understands alone

Cover everything except the heading. Could someone who has never heard of the
business say what it does, or what this section is about? If not, rewrite it.

A heading passes when it names at least two of these:

- **What** the business sells (billboards, haircuts, plumbing repairs)
- **Who** it is for (Gauteng brands, government buyers, new parents)
- **Where** (Kagiso, the West Rand, Gauteng, Bath Road)
- **The benefit or the fact** (counts at 135% for B-BBEE, open until 8pm)

Write it in sentence case, as words a person would say out loud. A heading may
be a full sentence or a plain topic name ("Billboard prices and sizes"). It may
not be a fragment that only makes sense after you read the section.

**Length follows meaning.** Most good headings run 4 to 12 words. Use the words
the meaning needs, then cut the ones it does not. NN/g measured BBC headlines
at about five words, and those five words always named the subject. If the
display size cannot hold a plain sentence in two or three lines, change the
size or the measure. Do not clip the sentence.

### 2. The hero passes the five-second test

Within the first screen, without scrolling, a visitor can answer three
questions (Donald Miller calls this the "grunt test"):

1. What do you sell?
2. Why should I care (the benefit, or the fact that sets you apart)?
3. How do I start?

The `<h1>` carries question 1 and usually question 2 in words. The supporting
line carries the detail. The primary button carries question 3.

**Hero formula options for a local service business.** Pick one and fill it
with facts from the brief:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| [Service] in/across [place] | Billboard, taxi and ATM-screen advertising across Gauteng |
| [Service] for [audience] in [place] | Outdoor advertising for brands that sell in Gauteng's townships |
| [Outcome] with [service] | Put your brand on Gauteng's busiest taxi routes |
| [Service], [key differentiator] | Billboards and taxi branding from a Level 1 B-BBEE supplier |
| [Place]'s [service] since [year] | Kagiso's outdoor advertising company since 2016 (only if the brief gives the year) |

A brand name alone is not a hero headline. If the design sets the name huge,
the next line down is the plain statement, set large enough to read in the
first screen, and it is the `<h1>`.

**Display type and the decode test.** A giant decorative word is allowed when
it is a concrete noun from the business (a place, a service, a product name)
and a real heading sitting beside it says what the business does. Mark the
decorative word `aria-hidden="true"` and keep the sentence as the heading.
"Gauteng" set 300px tall next to "Billboard and taxi advertising across
Gauteng" works. "Seen." set 300px tall works for nobody.

### 3. Section headings name the topic plainly

A visitor scanning the page reads headings only. Read them in order, alone,
top to bottom. They should work as a summary of the business:

> Billboard, taxi and ATM-screen advertising across Gauteng
> Five ways to put your brand in front of Gauteng commuters
> SARS, Mukuru and the Gauteng government have advertised with us
> Spend with us counts at 135% on your B-BBEE scorecard
> Questions about prices, sites and B-BBEE
> Get a quote on WhatsApp

Compare the shipped set: "Seen across Gauteng. / Five formats. / Already
booked. / Level 1 B-BBEE. / Before you book." Read alone, that list tells you
nothing.

Plain conventional headings are fine when the section holds what they say:
"Frequently asked questions", "Prices", "Opening hours", "Contact us". A plain
generic label still beats a cryptic one.

### 4. Body copy

- **Concrete nouns.** Name the thing: "roadside billboards on the R41",
  "ATM screens in shopping centres". Avoid "placements", "solutions",
  "offerings".
- **Numbers from the brief.** Prices, sizes, counts, years, hours, response
  times. If the brief has no number, write the sentence without one.
- **Active voice, with a person doing the thing.** "We print and install the
  board" beats "Boards are printed and installed".
- **Second person.** Talk to the reader as "you". Call the business "we" or
  by its name.
- **One idea per sentence.** Average 12 to 20 words. Split anything over 25
  words (the GOV.UK limit).
- **Reading age about 12, grade 6 to 8.** GOV.UK writes for a reading age of
  9 for the general public; a business buyer can take slightly more, and no
  more than that. Short common words win: "use" over "utilise", "help" over
  "facilitate", "about" over "approximately".
- **Front-load.** Put the most useful words at the start of each sentence,
  paragraph and list item.
- **Say how it works.** Steps, timing, what the customer sends, what they get
  back. "Send us the area and your dates. We reply with sites and prices the
  same day." Only state a timing the brief supports.
- **Write like the owner talks.** Use the owner's own words from the brief,
  WhatsApp messages or Facebook posts where you have them. Local terms the
  customer uses ("taxi rank", "spaza") are plain language to them.

### 5. Buttons and links: verb + object (+ outcome)

The label says what happens when you press it.

| Slop | Plain |
|---|---|
| Get started | Get a billboard quote |
| Learn more | See the five ad formats |
| Contact us | WhatsApp us for prices |
| Submit | Send my booking request |
| Let's talk | Call Clement on 072 … (use the real number from the brief) |
| Book now | Book a taxi-branding campaign |

Under the primary button, one line says what happens next, if the brief
supports it: "We reply on WhatsApp within a working day."

### 6. Facts you do not have

**Leave them out.** Never invent a number, a client, a quote, a year, an award,
a certification, a rating or a review. Write the sentence without the fact,
or leave a clearly marked slot and list it in `NOTES.md` for the owner to fill.
A plausible fake fact is worse than a missing one. The placeholder rules in
`content-and-copy.md` (Names, faces, and facts) still apply.

### 7. Labels, captions and micro-copy follow the same rules

An eyebrow, a marquee, a caption and an image `alt` all pass the decode test.
"Now showing" over a photo of a billboard fails. "Billboard on the N12 near
Lenasia, booked by Mukuru" passes, if the brief says so.

## Banned words and phrases

Each one has a replacement. If no replacement fits, the sentence had no fact in
it. Find the fact or delete the sentence.

## The decode test

Run it on every heading, label and button. It takes two minutes.

1. Copy every visible heading (`h1`–`h3`), eyebrow, marquee string and button
   label into a plain list, in page order, with nothing else.
2. Read each line to someone who has never seen the business. If nobody is
   available, read it as that person: a buyer who landed from a Google search,
   on a phone, half paying attention.
3. For each line, ask: "What does this business do?" and "What is this section
   about?" If the line does not answer at least one of them, it fails.
4. Read the whole list top to bottom. It should work as a summary of the
   business. If someone could not write the company's one-line description
   from the headings alone, the page fails.

`audit-page.mjs` runs a rough version of step 3 and reports `headline-cryptic`
as a WARN. It only catches the obvious shapes: under four words, or a short
verbless fragment ending in a full stop. Passing it says nothing about whether
the heading is clear. You still read the list.

## Copy review checklist

Run this in the copy pass, after the page renders and before the audit loop.

- [ ] Every visible string pulled into one list (headings, body, buttons,
      labels, captions, alt text, marquee, FAQ, footer, `<title>`)
- [ ] Hero passes the five-second test: what, why, how to start
- [ ] The `<h1>` names the service and the place or audience, in words
- [ ] Every heading passes the decode test on its own
- [ ] The headings, read top to bottom alone, describe the business
- [ ] No heading is a verbless fragment ending in a full stop
- [ ] No heading under four words, unless it is a plain conventional label
      ("Frequently asked questions", "Opening hours", "Contact us")
- [ ] Zero words or phrases from the banned lists (search the source)
- [ ] Zero banned structures, zero em dashes
- [ ] Sentence case on every heading and button
- [ ] Every button reads verb + object, and none says "Learn more",
      "Get started" or "Submit"
- [ ] One line after the primary button says what happens next
- [ ] Average sentence 12–20 words, none over 25
- [ ] Every number, client, year and claim traced to the brief; anything
      missing is left out or listed in `NOTES.md`
- [ ] Swap test: replace the business name with a competitor's. Any sentence
      that still works gets a fact from the brief, or goes

## Scoring rubric

Score each section of the page pass or fail on six checks. A section passes
only when all six pass. The page passes only when every section passes and the
hero passes the five-second test. There is no partial credit: one cryptic
heading on a page of clear ones still costs the reader that section.

| Check | Pass | Fail |
|---|---|---|
| **Decode** | The heading alone tells a stranger the topic or what the business does | The heading needs the section to make sense |
| **Specific** | The section carries at least one concrete fact from the brief (a service, place, client, number, step) | The section would read the same on a competitor's site |
| **Plain words** | Zero banned words or phrases | Any banned word or phrase |
| **Structure** | Zero banned structures; sentences average 12–20 words | Any fragment heading, tricolon, negative parallelism, em dash, or a sentence over 25 words |
| **Action** | Any button or link says verb + object; the next step is clear | "Learn more", "Get started", a bare imperative, or no way forward from a selling section |
| **Truth** | Every fact traces to the brief | Any invented number, client, rating, year or quote |

Post the scorecard in chat as a table: one row per section, six columns, and
the rewritten line for every fail. Fix every fail, then run the audit loop.
